Moving country is hard. Moving with children is twice as hard. The fear is
usually the same: that your child will not make friends, that the language will
be a wall, that the level will be too high or too low.

Start with what is working in your favour.

## Six to eleven is the best age for the move

- **The language arrives on its own.** At this age, within a few months your
  child understands the teacher, and within a year usually speaks better than
  their parents.
- **Academic pressure is low.** Primaria focuses on teamwork, projects and
  getting along.
- **Friendship groups are not fixed yet.** The rigid dynamics of adolescence have
  not set in, and a new child integrates far more easily.

## The three types of school

This is the distinction that confuses arriving families most.

### Públicos

Fully state-funded.

- **Cost:** free. You pay for the canteen, after-school activities and, depending
  on the region, books.
- **Environment:** full immersion. Your children will be in class with the
  neighbourhood kids.
- **Admission:** by points, weighted heavily towards **proximity to your address**
  (you need to be registered as resident) and having siblings already at the
  school.

**Who it suits:** anyone planning to stay, who wants real integration.

### Privados

Independent. Many are international schools — British, American, German — or
offer the International Baccalaureate.

- **Cost:** high. Monthly fees vary enormously by city and school, so ask for the
  full breakdown: registration, monthly fee, canteen, transport and materials are
  usually billed separately.
- **Curriculum:** often foreign or bilingual.

**Who it suits:** families who want to keep English as the main language, or who
move countries every few years and need curricular continuity.

### Concertados

The hybrid: private schools with partial public funding.

- **Cost:** tuition is formally free, but there are monthly "voluntary"
  contributions, plus canteen and uniform. Ask for the actual figure — it varies
  a great deal.
- **Ownership:** many belong to religious organisations. Religious education
  exists; how present it is varies by school and is worth asking about directly.

**Who it suits:** families looking for a middle ground on cost and expectations.

## Language: the fear that least often comes true

It is parents' number one worry, and the one that materialises least.

1. **There are no separate classes for foreign children.** Your child sits with
   everyone else from day one. It sounds harsh, and it is what works best.
2. **Watch out for co-official languages.** In Catalonia, Valencia, the Basque
   Country and Galicia there are two languages of instruction. In Barcelona a
   good share of subjects are taught in Catalan. Children this age take both in
   their stride, but you want to know **before** choosing a city.
3. **English depends heavily on the school.** Do not assume the level: ask how
   many hours a week and in which subjects.

## Ten questions for the head teacher

When you visit, ask freely. You are entrusting them with the most important thing
you have.

1. How do you support a newly arrived foreign child? Is there a settling-in tutor?
2. What is the average class size?
3. What is your protocol for conflict and bullying?
4. How much homework is set?
5. Are tablets used, or textbooks only?
6. Does the canteen have its own kitchen, or is it catered?
7. What after-school activities are there, and what do they cost?
8. How do you communicate with families?
9. Is there a uniform, and where is it bought?
10. Is there a school bus route?

## Deadlines and paperwork

For públicos and concertados, applications usually open **in spring** — March to
May, depending on the region — for the following school year. Privados admit
year-round while places last.

Usual documents: parents' and child's passports, NIE once you have one, proof of
local registration, family book or birth certificate, and academic records from
the previous school.

## And before all of that: the academic file

If your child brings qualifications from another country, one step comes first:
**having them formally recognised**, so the Spanish system can determine which
year they enter. Without that, the school cannot place them with confidence.

That procedure has its own timelines, and it is worth starting it before the
school search, not after.
