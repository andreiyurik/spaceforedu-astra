France was among the first countries to ratify the Hague Convention — **in force
since 24 January 1965** — so the paperwork route has been running for decades.
But there is a common misconception to clear up first.

## Both being in the EU does not exempt you from the apostille

**Regulation (EU) 2016/1191** abolishes the apostille between Union countries for
**civil-status documents**: birth, marriage, death, domicile, nationality, absence
of a criminal record.

**Academic qualifications fall outside that scope.** Your baccalauréat still needs
an apostille for the Spanish procedure.

Plenty of people assume the opposite, submit an unapostilled file, and receive a
correction request that costs them months.

## The documents

1. **Diplôme du baccalauréat**, apostilled.
2. **Relevé de notes** for the years to be recognised, apostilled.
3. **Valid ID**.
4. **Sworn translation into Spanish** of both academic documents, by a translator
   accredited by the Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs.

## The right order

**Apostille first, translate second.** The apostille certifies the signature on
the original; translate first and you may end up with a sworn translation — which
you pay for — of a document that had not yet been apostilled.

## Timelines

Three months officially from a complete file. **Four to eight** in reality, and
the difference is correction requests.

## You can enrol first

The **conditional enrolment slip** (article 15.2 of Royal Decree 104/1988) lets
you **enrol on the same terms as if recognition had been granted**, with your file
still in progress.

The warning goes in full: if the final decision does not come out as requested,
**your enrolment is annulled — and so are any exams you had already passed**.
