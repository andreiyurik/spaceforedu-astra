If you finished secondary school in Colombia and want to study in Spain, your
qualification does not count here automatically: it has to be formally
recognised. This guide covers what you actually need, in the order you need it.

## First: Colombia does use the apostille

**Colombia has been a party to the Hague Convention since 30 January 2001.**

That spares you the slowest and most expensive route. You do not need consular
legalisation — the trek through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and then the
Spanish consulate. An **apostille** is enough, and you can get it online from
Colombia.

## The four documents

1. **Acta de grado or bachillerato diploma**, apostilled.
2. **Transcript of grades** for the years you want recognised, apostilled.
3. **Valid ID** (cédula or passport).
4. **Sworn translation into Spanish** — only if a document is not already in
   Spanish. For Colombia this is usually unnecessary, and plenty of people pay
   for it anyway.

The first two are the ones that need the apostille. Your ID does not.

## How to apostille, concretely

Colombia's Cancillería does it online:

1. Go to the apostille portal of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs.
2. Register the document and pay the fee.
3. You receive the apostille electronically, with a verification code.

**One warning that saves weeks:** apostille the document **before** translating
it, if you need a translation at all. The apostille certifies the signature on
the original; translate first and you may end up with a translation of something
that had not yet been apostilled, and have to start over.

**Another:** the acta de grado must be issued by the school and registered as
required. An unregistered acta cannot be apostilled, and that is usually where
people discover they have steps missing.

## How long it really takes

The official decision period is three months from the moment the file is
complete. In practice, for well-prepared secondary-school files, what we see runs
from **four to eight months**.

The difference is almost never the Ministry. It is how many times you are asked
to correct something. A file that arrives complete the first time is resolved far
sooner than one that arrives half-built and collects request after request.

## What almost nobody tells you: you can enrol first

This is the part that changes an entire academic year.

There is a **conditional enrolment slip** (*volante de inscripción condicional*),
set out in article 15.2 of Royal Decree 104/1988. It lets you **enrol at
university on the same terms as if recognition had already been granted**, while
your file is still being processed.

In other words: you do not lose the year waiting for a decision.

**Now the warning, which matters just as much.** The slip is not early
recognition. If the final decision does not come out as requested, **your
enrolment is annulled — and so are any exams you had already passed**. It is not
a safe conduct. It is a bridge, and you should know the bridge can fall.

That is exactly why it matters that the file is built properly from the start.

## The three mistakes we see most

1. **Translating before apostilling.** Everything has to be redone.
2. **Apostilling the diploma but not the transcript.** The file arrives
   incomplete and opens with a correction request.
3. **Submitting an unregistered acta de grado.** It cannot be apostilled, and
   this surfaces late.
