If you finished secondary school in Venezuela and want to study in Spain, the
procedure is called *homologación* — and yes, your apostille counts.

## Venezuela does have the apostille, and has for a long time

**Venezuela has been a party to the Hague Convention since 16 March 1999**, with
no objections from any State.

Worth spelling out, because a fair amount of misinformation circulates on this.
You do not need consular legalisation: the apostille from your country is enough.

## The documents

1. **Bachillerato certificate**, apostilled.
2. **Transcript of grades** for the years to be recognised, apostilled.
3. **Valid ID**.

**Before you apostille, check one thing:** in many systems, school documents need
prior endorsement or certification from the education authority before they can be
apostilled. If your certificate has not been through that step, the apostille
cannot be issued. It is where most people get stuck, and it surfaces late.

**Two specific warnings that save repeat work:**

- Apostille **before** translating, if you need a translation at all. The
  apostille certifies the signature on the original; translate first and you may
  end up translating a document that was not yet apostilled.
- Check that your name appears **exactly the same** on the certificate, the
  transcript and your passport. One accent out of place or an abbreviated surname
  triggers a correction request — and each request restarts the clock.

## How long it really takes

Official period: three months from a complete file. Reality for well-prepared
secondary files: **four to eight months**.

## Do not lose the year waiting

There is a **conditional enrolment slip** (article 15.2 of Royal Decree 104/1988):
it lets you **enrol on the same terms as if recognition had already been granted**,
while your file is still open.

The warning almost nobody gives you: if the final decision does not come out as
requested, **your enrolment is annulled — and so are any exams you had already
passed**. It is a useful bridge, not a guarantee.
