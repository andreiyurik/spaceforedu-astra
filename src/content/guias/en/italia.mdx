There is one misconception that costs people real time, so let me start there.

## "We're both in the EU, so no apostille" — not quite

**Regulation (EU) 2016/1191** does abolish the apostille between Union countries.
But its scope is **civil-status documents**: birth, marriage, death, domicile,
nationality, absence of a criminal record.

**Academic qualifications are not in scope.** Your Italian diploma still needs an
apostille for the Spanish procedure.

The good news: **Italy has been a party to the Hague Convention since 11 February
1978**, with no objections. The mechanism exists and is straightforward.

## The documents

1. **Diploma di maturità**, apostilled.
2. **Transcript of grades** for the years to be recognised, apostilled.
3. **Valid ID**.
4. **Sworn translation into Spanish** of both academic documents. This one is
   genuinely required: Italian is not an official language in Spain, however
   close the two may look.

## Order matters

**Apostille first, translate second.** The apostille certifies the signature on
the original. Translate first and you risk holding a sworn translation of a
document that had not yet been apostilled — and paying for it twice.

The translation must be **sworn**, by a translator accredited by the Spanish
Ministry of Foreign Affairs. An ordinary translation, however good, will not do.

## Timelines

Three months officially from a complete file; **four to eight** in practice.
Correction requests are what stretch it, not the nationality of the file.

## You can enrol before the decision

The **conditional enrolment slip** (article 15.2 of Royal Decree 104/1988) lets
you **enrol on the same terms as if recognition had already been granted**, with
your file still in progress.

And the catch, in full: if the final decision does not come out as requested,
**your enrolment is annulled — and so are any exams you had already passed**.
